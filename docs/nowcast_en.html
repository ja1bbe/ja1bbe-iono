How to read this table

The bearing is where to point your antenna; “darkness” and “aurora” hint at how easily the path will carry.

DetailsLow-band signals are absorbed in the daytime D layer, so the darker the path, the better they tend to propagate. In the auroral zone, precipitating electrons increase absorption in the D and E layers, and 160m and 80m signals in particular can weaken greatly. Over North America the maximum geomagnetic latitude is higher than the geographic latitude because the geomagnetic pole is nearby. If your rotator is referenced to magnetic north, correct for the local magnetic declination.

Data and assumptionsBearings are great-circle bearings from true north, calculated from the centre of your grid locator. A 4-character grid covers about 180 km east–west and 110 km north–south, so a 6-character grid (e.g. FN31pr) is more accurate. “Darkness” is the fraction of points on the path where the sun is −6° or lower. “Maximum geomagnetic latitude along the path” uses a dipole approximation. Aurora probability is the NOAA OVATION estimate 30–90 minutes ahead at the highest point on the path (30% or more: “crossing”, 10–30%: “edge”). This is an estimate of auroral activity, not a direct probability of signal absorption or QSO failure.

Enter a callsign (e.g. JA1BBE, ZF2AA) or a grid locator (e.g. PM95, FK89ab) and press "Show path" to see the bearing, distance, maximum geomagnetic latitude along the path, aurora probability, path darkness and an assessment for the short and long paths. For a callsign, the representative position of its DXCC entity is used. When the short path crosses the auroral zone, bearings up to 60° to either side are searched for a path that avoids it (approximated as a path that bends on the way) and shown as a purple dotted line. This is a model-based alternative path estimate, not a confirmed propagation route, and it does not guarantee a contact.

4Evidence

The space-weather and ionospheric values behind the outlooks above.

Current valuesObserved

Bt is the strength of the solar-wind magnetic field (regardless of direction) and is the upper limit of how far Bz can turn south. Bt itself does not affect radio signals directly, but a large Bt (roughly 10 nT or more) means there is plenty of "fuel" for a disturbance: if the field rotates and Bz turns south, it can approach the size of Bt. When Bt is large and Bz is south, the geomagnetic field is likely to be disturbed — watch high-latitude paths, especially on the low bands. With a large Bt but Bz staying north, a major disturbance is unlikely.

The forecast is from the NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center and is based mainly on expected CME arrivals and recurring high-speed solar wind (about 27-day period). CME arrival times are generally uncertain by about half a day, so read the forecast times with a margin. A forecast of Kp 5 or more (G1 or more) means that paths over high latitudes may weaken. Ionosphere over Japan (Kokubunji, NICT) — JA side, for referenceObserved

Virtual height (last 6 hours, km)
foF2 (last 6 hours, MHz)

Virtual height is the reflection height derived from the echo delay; h'F is used when h'F2 is not available (e.g. at night). A sudden rise can precede a disturbance. As a rough guide, the MUF for a 3,000 km hop is often estimated as foF2 × 3; the actual value varies with ionospheric conditions and propagation geometry. These values are for the ionosphere directly above Kokubunji, Tokyo — the Japanese end of a path to JA — not the state along your whole path. NICT publishes the data once a day (around 11:30 UTC), so during the day they may be several hours old. Values include automatic processing.

Source: National Institute of Information and Communications Technology (NICT), ionospheric observation data https://wdc.nict.go.jp/Ionosphere/index.html

The band outlooks at the top are general tendencies for Japan (Tokyo) based on day and night, SFI and geomagnetic conditions; the path tools work for any pair of stations. None of this guarantees actual conditions. Low-band propagation also depends strongly on day and night, D-layer absorption and the noise at both stations. The final word is always actual reception and QSOs.
